Global oil prices climbed above $100 a barrel on Wednesday as escalating conflict in the Middle East renewed concerns about energy supplies from the region.

Benchmark Brent crude reached an intraday high of $100.19 a barrel, crossing the $100 threshold for the first time since July 24. It later traded just below that level.

US West Texas Intermediate crude also rose, reaching above $94 a barrel during Wednesday's trading.

## Why oil prices are rising

The latest increase followed a fresh escalation in the Middle East, including attacks affecting energy facilities and shipping.

Iran-backed Houthis have attacked Saudi energy infrastructure, while renewed US-Iran hostilities have increased concerns about shipping through the Gulf.

Several merchant vessels were also reported hit during military activity in the northern Gulf and Gulf of Oman on Wednesday.

The Strait of Hormuz remains particularly important to global energy markets because disruption there can restrict the movement of oil from major Gulf producers.

## What it means for Pakistan

Pakistan is heavily exposed to changes in international energy prices because it imports a substantial share of the petroleum products and crude oil used in the country.

Higher international prices do not automatically translate into an identical increase at Pakistani fuel pumps. Domestic prices are also affected by exchange rates, taxes, levies, margins and the government's pricing mechanism.

But sustained increases in global crude prices can raise Pakistan's import costs and put additional pressure on domestic fuel prices.

The latest international move comes as Pakistan has already raised petrol to Rs364.35 per litre and high-speed diesel to Rs385.95 per litre for September 9.

That makes developments in global oil markets especially important for Pakistani households, transport operators and businesses.

## Inflation is another risk

Expensive energy can affect more than petrol and diesel prices.

Higher transport and production costs can eventually feed into the prices of goods and services, while a larger energy import bill can add pressure to countries that depend heavily on imported fuel.

Global financial markets were also unsettled on Wednesday as investors considered whether higher energy costs could make inflation harder for central banks to control.

## What happens next

The key question is whether oil remains around or above $100 or retreats as geopolitical conditions change.

A brief spike would have different consequences from a sustained period of elevated prices.

For Pakistan, the direction of international crude prices, the rupee's exchange rate and the government's domestic pricing decisions will all matter for future fuel costs.
