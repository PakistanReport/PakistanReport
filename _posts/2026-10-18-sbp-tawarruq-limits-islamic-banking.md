The State Bank of Pakistan has introduced a detailed framework for Tawarruq-based transactions in Islamic finance, including an overall exposure ceiling of **5% of an institution's gross financing** and a separate **10% ceiling for Tawarruq-based consumer financing**, with specified exceptions.

The requirements accompany [IFPD Circular No. 03 of 2026, issued October 16](https://www.sbp.org.pk/circulars/ifpd-circular-no-03-of-2026). The circular adopts the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions' Shariah Standard No. 30 on Monetization (Tawarruq), subject to Pakistan-specific clarifications, and says it takes effect immediately.

The most consequential details are in two linked official documents: [Appendix A](https://www.sbp.org.pk/assets/documents/circulars/IFPD-2026-C3-Appendix-A.pdf), which sets out modifications and clarifications to the adopted standard, and [Annexure I](https://www.sbp.org.pk/assets/documents/circulars/IFPD-2026-C3-Annexure-I.pdf), which specifies how institutions must establish a genuine need, or *Ḥajah*, for Tawarruq transactions.

## What the exposure limits mean

Section 2.5 of Annexure I requires Tawarruq transactions to remain within **5% of an Islamic financial institution's gross financing**, counting consumer-financing and treasury transactions when determining overall exposure. It separately limits Tawarruq-based consumer financing to **10% of total consumer financing**, excluding credit-card products issued under Tawarruq from that specific consumer limit.

A one-time conversion of conventional loans and advances into Islamic financing, including commodity financing, is exempt from the stated limits. These percentages are **portfolio exposure ceilings**, not interest rates, customer charges or a limit on the size of an individual loan.

The limits also do not mean Tawarruq is prohibited. Rather, the framework restricts its scale and specifies conditions under which institutions may use it.

## Documented need, not routine convenience

The guidelines define *Ḥajah* as a pressing need whose non-fulfilment would cause hardship or difficulty, short of loss of life or property. They say the principle must be applied as an exception, not as a permanent business model or simply to meet profitability targets.

Before invoking it, institutions must document why other practicable Shariah-compliant products are unavailable. They must keep written justification and an eligibility assessment, explain alternative products and costs to customers, and obtain evidence showing how financing proceeds were actually used.

The guidelines also prohibit using Tawarruq to roll over financing merely to extend its term or to channel funds into interest-bearing activities. These are explicit restrictions in Annexure I, rather than assumptions about the purpose of Islamic banking.

## Rules for commodities and intermediaries

Tawarruq generally involves acquiring a commodity on deferred payment terms and selling it to a different party for immediate cash. Appendix A requires a genuine underlying commodity, identifiable assets and actual or constructive possession. It also restricts arrangements that would return the commodity to the original seller or improperly link purchase and resale contracts.

Under the guidelines, a customer should be able to take possession or arrange an independent selling agent. An institution may act as the customer's selling agent only in exceptional circumstances of necessity, subject to the relevant Shariah board or adviser approval.

Appendix A also restricts using commodity transactions to provide liquidity to conventional banks where the proceeds would support interest-based activity.

## Oversight and enforcement

The rules require board-approved policies and oversight by Shariah boards or advisers. Management must report overall Tawarruq exposure to the board semi-annually, while Shariah compliance reporting and internal Shariah audits are required quarterly.

The circular addresses Islamic banks, Islamic banking subsidiaries and conventional banks with Islamic banking branches. Annexure I describes a broader operational scope covering Islamic finance products offered by microfinance banks, development finance institutions and digital banks as well.

The central bank warns that failure to comply can lead to penalties under the Banking Companies Ordinance, 1962. Annexure I states that prior circular or letter instructions and approvals concerning Tawarruq transactions are revoked; other applicable regulations continue to apply.
