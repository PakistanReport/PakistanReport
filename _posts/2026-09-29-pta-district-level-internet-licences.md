**ISLAMABAD —** The Pakistan Telecommunication Authority has issued around 300 district-level Class Licences for internet services under a framework intended to bring more local operators into the broadband market and encourage fibre deployment beyond Pakistan’s largest urban centres.

Recent reports citing PTA officials put the number at about 300, although some accounts describe it as “nearly 300”. The more cautious formulation is therefore that the regulator has issued around 300 district-level licences rather than treating 300 as an independently verified exact total.

A significant share of recent licences has reportedly been issued for districts in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, where the framework is intended to support expansion by smaller and locally focused service providers.

### One district rather than a nationwide footprint

The district-level model lowers the geographic scale at which a company can enter the internet-service market. Instead of requiring an operator to build a province-wide or national business, a Class Licence can authorise service within a specific district.

Reports on the licensing conditions say each licence is valid for 10 years. The initial licence fee is Rs300,000, with an annual fee of Rs100,000 in the first year. Licence holders are required to begin commercial service within one year and meet initial subscriber requirements.

The framework does not mean that every company receiving a licence has already built a functioning broadband network. A licence provides regulatory permission to operate subject to its conditions; deployment still requires investment in fibre, last-mile infrastructure, equipment, bandwidth and customer acquisition.

### Fibre and last-mile connectivity

The policy is aimed particularly at fixed broadband and fibre connectivity. Smaller district-based providers can extend last-mile networks in areas where large national operators may have less commercial incentive to invest.

Under the reported conditions, district-level providers must obtain bandwidth from duly licensed operators. That preserves a regulated link between local internet service providers and the upstream networks supplying connectivity.

The potential benefit is greater competition at district level and more investment in areas with limited fixed broadband availability. The outcome will depend on how many licensees actually launch services, the quality and price of those services, and whether fibre deployment reaches communities that are currently underserved.

### Licence count is only the first measure

The number of licences is an indicator of market entry, not of completed connectivity. A fuller assessment of the programme will require data on commercial launches, active subscribers, fibre kilometres deployed, service quality and geographic coverage.

For now, the development shows that PTA’s district-level licensing approach is attracting a substantial number of applicants. If those licences translate into operating networks, the model could widen the pool of broadband providers serving individual districts without requiring each entrant to build at national scale.
