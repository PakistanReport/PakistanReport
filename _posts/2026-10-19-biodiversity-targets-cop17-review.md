Countries have made substantial progress in establishing biodiversity commitments, but fewer than half of the national targets assessed by governments are on track, according to a United Nations review released on 18 October ahead of the COP17 conference in Armenia.

The *State of Biodiversity Action* assessment examines implementation of the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, agreed in 2022. It contains 23 targets for 2030. The central finding is a gap between **setting targets and making measurable progress towards them**.

The Convention on Biological Diversity says nearly 90% of parties have established national biodiversity targets. Yet fewer than half of the national targets assessed by parties are on track. The two figures measure different things: one concerns the adoption of targets by countries, while the other concerns progress against individual targets assessed by those countries.

## A commitment is not an outcome

A government can adopt a conservation goal without having completed the measures needed to achieve it. The global review points to shortcomings in financing, policy coordination and the information required to measure progress.

**The percentages cannot legitimately be subtracted to calculate the share of countries failing.** The denominator for the first figure is parties to the biodiversity convention; the denominator for the second is assessed national targets. Treating them as equivalent would exaggerate what the UN report establishes.

The review also finds that countries' national targets address an average of 22 of the framework's 23 global targets, but the scope of those national commitments is often narrower than the corresponding global ambition. Broad coverage on paper therefore does not guarantee that the substantive requirements have been adopted.

## Conservation pledges fall short of the 30% goal

The framework includes a global goal to conserve 30% of land and sea by 2030. The UN says current national pledges fall well short of that ambition.

This is a gap in commitments as well as delivery. Even where a government has designated a protected area, the effectiveness of its protection and the condition of the ecosystem remain separate questions. The report's warning should not be read as evidence that every protected area is ineffective; it indicates that the aggregate commitments are insufficient.

Another significant gap concerns economic incentives. The review says many countries have set targets promoting incentives that benefit biodiversity, while fewer have committed to reducing subsidies and other incentives that harm it. These are distinct policy choices, and action on one does not necessarily offset the effects of the other.

## Where implementation is weakest

According to the Convention on Biological Diversity's assessment of implementation-related targets, two-thirds of national targets involving businesses and financial institutions show no or insufficient progress. It also says 70% of national targets related to sustainable consumption show insufficient progress.

These are **shares of relevant national targets**, not shares of companies, consumers or countries. They highlight how biodiversity policy depends on decisions beyond environment ministries, including investment, purchasing, agriculture and fiscal policy.

The same assessment reports that only about a quarter of national targets closely aligned with reforming harmful incentives are on track. It also identifies weaknesses in private finance and in the data required to assess results.

## What COP17 can establish

COP17 is scheduled for 19–30 October in Yerevan, Armenia. Governments will use the first global review of the framework to assess collective progress and consider how to accelerate implementation.

The review is an important benchmark, but it is not an independent audit of every country's ecological outcomes. Much of the assessment depends on national reporting, and the strength of evidence varies between targets.

The immediate test is whether governments can translate their existing commitments into specific policies, finance and credible monitoring before 2030. The report documents a substantial implementation gap; whether COP17 narrows it will require evidence from decisions and subsequent action, not merely conference declarations.

No country-level performance ranking has been inferred from the aggregate figures.
