Saudi Arabia's state-owned oil company Aramco has suffered damage at its headquarters complex in Dhahran after an attack on Tuesday, October 13, as fighting between the kingdom and Yemen's Houthi movement intensifies.

The Saudi Ministry of Energy acknowledged that an administrative building in the compound had been targeted and that a fire broke out. The ministry described the fire as limited and said there were no deaths or injuries, according to [Reuters' October 13 report](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/saudi-led-coalition-orders-evacuation-yemen-oil-facility-sanaa-ahead-operation-2026-10-13/).

Subsequent reporting on October 14 indicated that the physical damage was more extensive than the initial official description suggested.

Three people with direct knowledge of the incident told [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/aramcos-dhahran-hq-suffered-extensive-damage-tuesdays-attack-sources-say-2026-10-14/) that fire spread across several floors of an office building. The news agency reported that the upper floors, including an area housing the office of Aramco chief executive Amin Nasser, were severely damaged.

The [Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/c81a91347c00b17097c8fafde7e4eeec) separately cited two regional officials who said executive offices had been hit. These accounts rely on sources who were not authorised to speak publicly, and the precise extent of damage has not been established through a detailed public assessment by Aramco.

## Saudi authorities confirm attack, report no casualties

The energy ministry's statement confirmed an attack on the company's administrative premises and said authorities responded to the resulting fire. Its report of no casualties relates to the Aramco incident, not to the wider conflict.

Aramco's headquarters are in Dhahran in Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province, according to the company's [official contact information](https://www.aramco.com/en/global-contacts).

The headquarters complex hosts an important operations centre involved in overseeing oil production, processing and export infrastructure.

Reuters reported on October 14 that operations had continued, with some staff working remotely and certain functions moved to other offices. Those operational details were attributed to people familiar with the situation, rather than a formal company-wide statement.

There was no independently verified indication in the reporting reviewed for this article that the headquarters attack had halted Saudi oil production or exports.

## Houthis claim attacks on Saudi targets

Yemen's Houthi movement said on Wednesday that it had launched missiles and drones against Saudi targets, including Aramco facilities in Dhahran and Riyadh and King Khalid International Airport.

The Saudi-led coalition separately reported intercepting missiles and drones aimed at several locations, including Dhahran and the capital, Riyadh. The competing statements should not be read as independent confirmation of every weapon's flight path or impact.

The Houthis have described their attacks as retaliation for Saudi military operations against areas of Yemen under their control.

Saudi-led forces have also struck Houthi-controlled fuel and energy facilities, including sites around Sanaa, amid a renewed phase of the conflict.

The escalation has affected civilian areas as well as military and industrial targets. Saudi Civil Defence reported that falling debris from intercepted missiles injured four people and damaged homes in Riyadh, according to [Associated Press reporting](https://apnews.com/article/307d49abfe63ff66c99c318d94ca3e15).

## Implications for the energy sector

Aramco is central to Saudi Arabia's petroleum industry, and attacks on its headquarters can raise concerns about the security of the infrastructure and personnel needed to manage energy operations.

However, damage to an office building is not the same as damage to producing oilfields, refineries or export terminals. Any assessment of supply disruption requires evidence about those facilities and the company's actual output.

For Pakistan, which imports petroleum products and is exposed to international energy-market fluctuations, a sustained regional escalation could be economically significant. This is a potential risk, not evidence that the Dhahran incident has already increased Pakistan's import bill.

The immediate confirmed developments are the Saudi government's acknowledgement of the attack and fire, the absence of reported casualties at the Aramco building, and subsequent Reuters and AP accounts describing substantial damage to offices.

Further information from Aramco or Saudi authorities would be needed to establish the full cost of repairs, any longer-term operational consequences and whether additional protective measures are being taken.
