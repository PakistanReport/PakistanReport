Pakistan's stronger foreign-exchange reserves have given policymakers greater confidence in the country's ability to withstand external shocks. But higher energy costs, external debt obligations and uncertain global conditions remain significant tests of that stability.

State Bank of Pakistan Governor Jameel Ahmad highlighted the country's improved financial position during meetings with international investors held alongside the IMF and World Bank annual meetings in Bangkok in October.

The central bank reported foreign-exchange reserves of approximately $21.5 billion by October 9, compared with less than $3 billion in February 2023. The improvement provides a larger buffer against unexpected pressures on external payments, although the reserve balance alone does not determine how resilient the economy will be.

## Stronger reserves provide breathing room

Foreign-exchange reserves help Pakistan pay for imports, meet international obligations and manage periods of financial uncertainty.

The State Bank expects economic growth of between 3.5% and 4.5% in fiscal year 2026–27, following estimated growth of 3.7% in the previous financial year. It also projects the current-account deficit to remain within 1% of gross domestic product.

These are forecasts, not final outcomes. Actual performance will depend on domestic activity, external financing conditions and developments in international markets.

## External risks remain

Pakistan is particularly sensitive to changes in global oil prices because imported energy affects both the country's foreign-currency needs and domestic production costs.

A sustained rise in energy prices could increase the import bill and place renewed pressure on businesses and households. Debt repayments and refinancing requirements also make continued access to external funding important.

Pakistan's recent staff-level agreement with the International Monetary Fund represents progress in its financing programme, but the associated funds remain subject to IMF Executive Board approval.

## Stability is not the same as recovery

Improved reserves and a more manageable external account can reduce immediate financial vulnerabilities. They do not automatically bring down household expenses or guarantee stronger employment and investment.

Long-term resilience will depend on increasing exports, improving energy-sector performance, attracting productive investment and sustaining credible economic policies.

Pakistan has rebuilt an important financial buffer. The next challenge is converting that stability into lasting growth while preparing for the possibility of further global shocks.
