Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf has said it would resign from the national and provincial assemblies if governor’s rule or an emergency is imposed in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, adding another escalation point to tensions between the party and the federal government.

PTI’s Khyber Pakhtunkhwa president Junaid Akbar Khan made the warning during a television interview as debate continued over possible federal intervention in the province.

Akbar said PTI would not remain in the assemblies if either measure were imposed in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. His statement was conditional: no governor’s rule or emergency has been imposed in the province.

The warning comes amid a wider confrontation involving PTI’s protest campaign, disagreements between Islamabad and the provincial government and competing claims over political space and security policy.

Akbar argued that PTI had tried to find political middle ground but that its willingness to negotiate had been treated as weakness. He linked the party’s return to street protests to restrictions on political activity, disqualifications and other grievances.

He also said PTI founder Imran Khan remained a central priority for the party and maintained that the long march would not be called off simply at the government’s request.

Parliamentary Affairs Minister Tariq Fazal Chaudhry challenged the resignation warning and claimed that 36 PTI members of the National Assembly would refuse to resign if the party ordered its lawmakers to leave parliament.

That figure is a government claim and has not been independently established.

Akbar responded that PTI would proceed with resignations regardless of how many lawmakers declined to follow the party decision, saying the party could replace members who did not support its political position.

The exchange has revived questions over whether PTI could again use parliamentary resignations as part of a broader confrontation with the federal government.

Discussion of governor’s rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has intensified during the current political standoff, but statements from federal officials have not always pointed in the same direction.

Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi said in September that the government was consulting the law ministry about legal options, including governor’s rule, if PTI proceeded with its march. Information Minister Attaullah Tarar subsequently said the federal government had no intention of removing the provincial administration.

Other federal ministers have continued to discuss constitutional options that could become available if the situation deteriorated.

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Chief Minister Sohail Afridi has rejected warnings about governor’s rule or an emergency and has maintained that the provincial government would not retreat because of such threats.

Under Pakistan’s constitutional framework, federal intervention in a province is not an ordinary political step and would require the relevant constitutional conditions and procedures. Political discussion of the option does not itself mean that those requirements have been met or that a decision has been taken.

The immediate development is therefore PTI’s declared response to a possible future move rather than an announcement that governor’s rule is imminent.

The resignation warning nevertheless raises the stakes in the dispute because it links any federal intervention in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa to PTI’s continued participation in both provincial and national parliamentary politics.

For now, the party remains represented in the assemblies and the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa government remains in office. Whether the warning develops into an actual resignation process depends on steps that the federal government has not taken.
