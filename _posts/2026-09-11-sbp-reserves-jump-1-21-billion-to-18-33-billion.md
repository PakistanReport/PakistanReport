Foreign-exchange reserves held by the State Bank of Pakistan rose by $1.21 billion to $18.33 billion during the week ending September 4, according to [Business Recorder’s report quoting the central bank’s weekly statement](https://www.brecorder.com/news/40438880/sbp-held-foreign-exchange-reserves-surge-121bn-to-1833bn).

The report attributed the increase to government commercial loan proceeds. SBP’s [official indicators](https://www.sbp.org.pk/) put its reserves at $18,328.2 million and total liquid foreign-exchange reserves, including commercial-bank holdings, at $23,715.8 million.

The increase adds to Pakistan’s foreign-currency buffer. Its source also matters: borrowing brings funds into the country while creating obligations that must be managed later.

## What the figures show

Commercial banks held a separate $5,387.6 million in foreign-exchange reserves, according to SBP’s breakdown. Combined with the central bank’s holdings, that takes the total to approximately $23.72 billion.

The distinction between these components is important. The national total combines central-bank and commercial-bank holdings; it should not be described as money held entirely by SBP.

The reference date also matters. These are balances for September 4, rather than a measurement of reserves on September 10. The weekly update provides a snapshot of a completed reporting period.

## Why the source of the increase matters

A reserve balance shows the foreign currency held at a particular point. Understanding the change in that balance requires examining the inflows and outflows behind it.

The commercial-borrowing explanation reported from SBP’s statement means the latest increase should be assessed alongside the repayment obligations associated with that financing. It does not, by itself, establish an equivalent improvement in recurring foreign-currency earnings.

Borrowed funds can help meet immediate payments. Maintaining a stronger reserve position over time also depends on future inflows and the amount and timing of repayments.

The figures cited here do not identify the loan’s lender, interest rate or repayment schedule. Those details would be needed to assess the financing cost and when it will place demands on future foreign-currency resources.

## What it means for the economy

SBP’s [reserve-management framework](https://www.sbp.org.pk/our-operations/financial-market/exchange-rate-regime-and-fx-reserve-management) links decisions about reserves to maturities and payment obligations, with safety and liquidity among its core objectives.

A larger balance can provide more room to manage external payments. Its adequacy depends on the obligations it must cover, rather than the headline amount alone.

The increase also does not guarantee a particular exchange rate. SBP describes Pakistan’s system as market-based and flexible, with demand and supply determining the rate.

## What to watch next

Subsequent weekly releases will show whether the higher balance is maintained. The useful comparison will be both the size of reserves and the explanation for changes, including financing receipts and repayments.

The immediate improvement is a larger foreign-currency buffer. Its durability will depend on how incoming funds compare with payment obligations as they fall due.
