International oil prices fell on Tuesday as investors weighed improving crude export flows from the Middle East against continuing disruption risks linked to the regional conflict.

Reuters reported Brent crude futures at $103.32 a barrel at 1306 GMT, down 1.86%, while US West Texas Intermediate was at $90.65, down 2.11%. These were intraday prices rather than final settlement levels.

The decline followed signs that more crude was reaching international markets. Reuters reported increased exports from Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, with the resumption of operations linked to Saudi Arabia’s East-West Pipeline and renewed loadings from the Red Sea port of Yanbu helping increase available volumes.

Middle East crude flows were estimated at 12.8 million barrels per day in September, according to the Reuters report, the highest level since February.

That improvement has eased some immediate supply concerns, but it has not removed the geopolitical risk premium from the market. Shipping conditions remain more complicated than before the conflict, and the Strait of Hormuz remains central to global energy-market concerns.

Brent and WTI also remained on course for monthly gains despite Tuesday’s decline, reflecting how sharply energy prices have risen during the broader period of disruption.

For Pakistan, international oil prices are important because the country relies heavily on imported petroleum. Changes in crude and refined-product prices can affect the import bill and feed into domestic fuel-price calculations, transport costs and inflation.

However, a single intraday move in Brent or WTI does not determine Pakistan’s next petrol or diesel adjustment. Domestic prices are affected by several components, including international product prices, exchange rates, taxation, levies and the pricing period used by the government.

The latest market movement therefore represents a change in global conditions rather than a confirmed change in Pakistani pump prices.

The recovery in export flows is also important because the current disruption has forced producers and traders to use alternative routes and more complex logistics. Those arrangements can keep freight and handling costs elevated even when physical volumes improve.

Markets are additionally watching diplomatic contacts involving the United States and Iran. Separate discussions through mediators have resumed, but no settlement has been announced and uncertainty remains high.

Tuesday’s decline shows that additional barrels reaching the market can quickly ease price pressure. At the same time, oil remains elevated compared with levels before the latest phase of regional disruption, leaving import-dependent economies exposed to further volatility if supply conditions worsen again.
