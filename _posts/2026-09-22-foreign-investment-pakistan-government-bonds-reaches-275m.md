Foreign investors placed about $275 million in Pakistani government securities during the first 70 days of the current fiscal year, with a sizeable share of the inflows going into longer-term Pakistan Investment Bonds.

State Bank of Pakistan data reported on Tuesday show overseas investors placed $151.4 million in Treasury bills and $123.7 million in PIBs between July 1 and September 11. The two components total $275.1 million before rounding.

The composition of the inflows is notable because foreign portfolio investment in Pakistani government debt has often been concentrated in shorter-term Treasury bills. The latest figures show substantial participation in PIBs, which carry longer maturities.

Investors based in the United Arab Emirates accounted for about $100 million of the reported inflows, including $90 million in PIBs and $10 million in Treasury bills, according to the SBP figures reported by Dawn.

UK investors placed $60.7 million in Treasury bills. Luxembourg accounted for $19.4 million in T-bills and $25 million in PIBs, while US investors placed $23 million in Treasury bills and $8.6 million in PIBs. Bahrain accounted for about $30 million in Treasury-bill investment.

## Longer-term bonds draw foreign funds

The move into PIBs became particularly visible in early September. During the first 11 days of the month, foreign investors placed $49.7 million in PIBs compared with $12 million in Treasury bills, according to the reported central-bank data.

Pakistani government securities continue to offer relatively high yields, which can attract overseas investors prepared to take rupee, interest-rate and country risk. Market participants cited by Dawn pointed to yields of around 12% as one factor supporting demand.

The inflows should, however, be distinguished from foreign direct investment. Purchases of Treasury bills and PIBs are portfolio investment in government debt, not investment in factories, businesses or other productive assets.

Portfolio funds can also move in and out more quickly than direct investment. Returns for overseas investors depend not only on the yield offered by the securities but also on movements in the rupee, changes in domestic interest rates and perceptions of economic and geopolitical risk.

That distinction is particularly relevant during a period of heightened regional uncertainty. The latest figures demonstrate foreign demand for Pakistani government securities during the period covered by the data, but do not by themselves establish a permanent change in foreign investor sentiment.

## Why the composition matters

Treasury bills are short-term government securities, while Pakistan Investment Bonds generally carry longer maturities. A sustained increase in foreign participation in PIBs would therefore represent a different pattern from inflows concentrated mainly in short-duration instruments.

The State Bank tracks foreign portfolio flows through its foreign-investment statistics and Special Convertible Rupee Account data. Its economic-data portal shows updated foreign-investment datasets and daily and weekly SCRA information for September.

Foreign purchases of rupee-denominated securities bring foreign currency into Pakistan and can support external-account liquidity while the funds remain invested. The reverse is also true: portfolio outflows can create pressure when investors reduce exposure or repatriate funds.

The $275 million recorded between July 1 and September 11 is therefore best read as evidence of renewed foreign participation in domestic government debt, with the share directed towards longer-term PIBs providing the most notable feature of the latest data.

Whether that pattern continues will depend on yields, the exchange rate, monetary-policy expectations and investors' assessment of Pakistan's economic and regional risks over the coming months.
